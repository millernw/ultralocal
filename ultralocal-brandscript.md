## 0. The business in one paragraph

Ultra Local places free chalk-style daily menu boards inside high-traffic small businesses in northeast Indiana (pubs, restaurants, coffee shops, dentist and doctor offices, salons, and similar). The border of each board is made up of 4x6 ad spots sold to local service businesses and nonprofits for $625 per year. The host business gets a free, professionally built board to post specials and announcements, plus a say in which sponsors appear. Sponsors get a full year of being seen by their actual neighbors, in places those neighbors already spend time. Ultra Local is openly anti big-advertising and anti big-corporation: it exists to help small local businesses through small local advertising. Ultra Local is affiliated with Systematic Marketing (goSystematic.com), which handles billing, but it is presented as its own community brand.

# PART 1: BRANDSCRIPT

The site has three heroes, told in sequence, not in competition:

1. **The Local** (general visitor): arrives first, hears the umbrella story.
2. **The Host**: a small business with lots of foot traffic that gets a free board.
3. **The Sponsor**: a local service business or nonprofit that buys a spot on the board.

The homepage speaks to The Local first, then splits into two columns: Host on one side, Sponsor on the other. Each hero has its own BrandScript below.

## 1.1 Umbrella story (Hero 1: The Local)

**Character (what they want):** To see their town's small businesses thrive, and to keep their community feeling like theirs.

**Villain:** Big Advertising and the chains it feeds. Specifically:
- National chains and franchises that crowd out locally owned businesses
- Pay-to-play ad platforms that make local businesses rent their own neighbors' attention from a company in another state
- The endless scroll, where a local plumber's ad sits between a national brand and a video from 2,000 miles away

**External problem:** Local businesses are hard to find and easy to forget, while chains are everywhere.
**Internal problem:** They feel their town slowly turning into anywhere-USA and don't know how to help beyond "shop local" slogans.
**Philosophical problem:** Money spent in a town should build that town. Small businesses shouldn't have to pay giant corporations just to be seen by the people down the street.

**Guide:**
- Empathy: "We love small business. They're the heartbeat of our towns, and they're getting squeezed by corporations and franchises."
- Authority: see 1.4.

**Plan:** Spot a board. Support the businesses on it. Nominate the next spot.

**Call to action:**
- Direct (Local): Nominate a spot that should have a board
- Direct (split point): "I own a business with foot traffic" / "I want my business on a board"
- Transitional: See where the boards are

**Failure (stakes):** More chains, more empty storefronts, more money leaving the county, fewer familiar faces behind the counter.
**Success:** A town where you know who fixes your furnace, who owns the coffee shop, and who sponsors the Little League team, because you see their names every week in the places you already go.

**Identity transformation:** From someone who cares about their town to someone who actively keeps it local.

## 1.2 Host BrandScript (Hero 2)

**Character (what they want):** An easy, good-looking way to keep customers up to date on specials, events, and hours.

**Villain:** The information gap. Handwritten printer-paper signs taped to the door, a chalkboard nobody has time to redo, specials that never make it past the register.

**External problem:** They don't have a reliable tool for getting up-to-date information in front of patrons.
**Internal problem:** They're frustrated that customers miss specials and events, and a little embarrassed by makeshift signage.
**Philosophical problem:** A local business shouldn't have to pay for the tools that make its own place feel alive, especially when neighbors are happy to help cover it.

**Guide empathy:** "You're running a business, not a print shop. Keeping customers in the loop shouldn't be another job."
**Guide authority:** Sign and wide-format graphics background, so boards are built to look good and last. Hosts approve who appears.

**Plan (3 steps):**
1. **Apply.** Tell us about your space. We confirm you have the foot traffic to make it work for sponsors (target: 5,000+ visitors a month) and that the board fits where you want it.
2. **Make your Yes and No lists.** Tell us the local businesses you'd love to see on your board, and any you don't want in your place (competitors, anything that doesn't fit your crowd). You have a say.
3. **Use your board.** We install it and hand you a box of liquid chalk markers and an eraser. Post your daily specials, events, and news. Bonus good vibes if you share photos of it on social.

**Agreement plan (reduces fear):**
- Always free to host. No cost, ever.
- You choose who you'd love on the board, and who you don't.
- No competitors on your board.
- Professionally designed and printed, not a cheap poster.
- The only commitment is keeping the board up for one year.

**Call to action:**
- Direct: **Get a Free Board**
- Transitional: See what a board looks like / Host FAQ

**Failure:** Specials nobody hears about, events nobody shows up for, walls that say nothing.
**Success:** Customers read the board every visit, specials move, and your business becomes the spot where the neighborhood checks in. You also helped a dozen other local businesses get seen.

**Identity transformation:** From a busy owner with a messy signage problem to the neighborhood hub that helps other local businesses.

## 1.3 Sponsor BrandScript (Hero 3)

**Character (what they want):** To be the name locals think of first for what they do.

Emotional driver underneath: they want to feel proud when they walk into the local pub, restaurant, or dentist office and see their business on the wall, and to be seen as a community leader. **Lead with the external result ("be the name people already know"), let the pride carry the emotion. Do not lead with vanity.**

**Villain:** Pay-to-play advertising. The big corporate ad machine that needs to be fed every month to keep you visible, casts too wide a net, and shows your ad to people too far away to ever hire you. And with the local newspaper gone, there's no good way left to reach truly local people.

**External problem:** They're spending too much on complicated ad platforms and landing in front of the wrong people.
**Internal problem:** They feel like they're pouring money into a machine they don't understand, and they're not sure any of it is working.
**Philosophical problem:** A local business should be able to advertise to its neighbors without paying a corporation for permission.

**Guide empathy:** "You didn't start your business to become an ad-platform expert. You just want the people around you to know you're here."
**Guide authority:** Years in marketing, both big-budget and small-business. Co-founded a sign and wide-format graphics company. Bachelor's in graphic arts. Can design the ad for you.

**Plan (3 steps):**
1. **Pick your board.** Choose a location your customers already visit.
2. **Approve your ad.** Send us your 4x6 artwork or we'll design it for you. Add a QR code if you'd like.
3. **Enjoy a year of being seen.** Your ad stays up for 12 months. Before the next year, you'll get an invoice and decide whether to renew. Renewing? You can keep your ad or swap in a new one.

**Agreement plan:**
- $625 for the full year. That's about $52 a month, or roughly $1.71 a day.
- No bidding, no clicks, no algorithm. One price, one year.
- Guaranteed 12 months of consistent display.
- Only local businesses. No national brands, ever.
- Every host approves its sponsors, so you're welcomed, not tolerated.

**Objection to answer head-on:** "Does anyone actually look at these?"
- Answer: Boards only go in places with serious foot traffic (target 5,000+ visitors a month). People read a daily menu board on purpose, to see today's special. Your ad sits right next to the thing they came to look at, every visit, all year, and it can't be skipped or scrolled past. Optional QR code lets you see scans.

**Call to action:**
- Direct: **Claim Your Spot**
- Transitional: See available boards / Sponsor FAQ

**Founding offer framing (launch period):** Ultra Local is placing its first boards now. Frame early sponsors as **Founding Sponsors** and early hosts as **Founding Hosts**. This is status, not a discount (no price change unless Nathan adds one). Use for scarcity and community-leader identity: "Be one of the first names on the first boards in northeast Indiana."

**Failure:** Keep feeding the machine every month, stay invisible to the neighbors who'd actually hire you, and watch the franchise down the road become the default.
**Success:** "Oh, you're the one from the board at [local pub]." You're the familiar name in town for what you do, and people call you because they already feel like they know you.

**Identity transformation:** From renting attention from a corporate platform to being a recognized, go-to community business.

**One-liner (primary):**

**Short one-liner (hero subhead length):**

**Tagline options (pick one for the hero; A is recommended):**

# PART 3: DESIGN, ASSETS, AND BUILD

## 3.1 Visual direction

**Concept:** "Main Street after dark." A 1950s/60s neon diner and marquee feel, warm and fun, never kitschy. Think neon signs in a diner window, marquee bulbs, chalkboard menus, cream-colored paper menus, and hand-painted shop signs.

- Alternate dark sections (Night Navy, neon accents) with light sections (Cream, navy text) so long pages breathe and stay readable.
- The chalkboard menu board is the product. Use a chalkboard-style motif (dark board, cream "chalk" text, ad tiles around the frame) as a recurring visual device, especially in the hero and split section.
- Neon glow effects: headlines and logo only, on dark backgrounds. Never on body text.
- Marquee bulb borders for the Founding offer banner.
- Retro iconography: simple line icons with rounded ends, in the teal/pink/gold palette.
- Motion: subtle. A neon "flicker on" for the hero logo on load (once, respect `prefers-reduced-motion`), gentle hover lift on cards and buttons. No parallax, no scroll-jacking.

**Contrast rules (checked):**
- Body text: cream on navy (14:1) or navy on cream (14:1). Nothing else for paragraphs.
- On navy: teal (5.3:1) and gold (7.9:1) are fine for any text. Pink (4.2:1) is large or bold text only. All tube colors are fine.
- On cream: teal (2.7:1), pink (3.4:1), and gold (1.8:1) fail for body text. Use them as fills, borders, and icons on cream, not as text color. Pink may be used for headings 24px+ on cream.
- **Primary button:** gold background, navy text (7.9:1). **Secondary button:** transparent with cream or navy outline. Avoid white text on pink or teal buttons.
